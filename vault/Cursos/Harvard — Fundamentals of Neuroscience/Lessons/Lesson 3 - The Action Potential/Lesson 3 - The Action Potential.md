# Lesson 3 - The Action Potential

## Introduction to Lesson 3: The Action Potential
As we’ve alluded to previously, neurons can create electrical signals in the form of rapid changes in their membrane voltage. One stereotyped form of membrane voltage change is called the ‘action potential’, which is the subject of this lesson.

In previous lessons we’ve been concerned with the passive properties of the neuron, such as the passive flow of ions through leakage channels. However, now we will turn our attention towards a neuron’s active properties, specifically how a neuron responds to an electrical stimulation. We will spend time considering a new type of channel: the voltage-gated channel. By discussing these different properties, both active and passive, we will come to understand the complex molecular events that underlie the most fundamental unit of communication between neurons.

---

## The Fundamentals of the Action Potential
Neurons communicate with each other using rapid changes in their membrane potentials. These fast changes are called ‘action potentials’ and can be sent down an axon in order to send an electrical signal from one neuron to the next. As you’ll see in the video, another word for an action potential is a ‘spike,’ given the shape that we see in a voltage trace for a neuron when an action potential occurs. This idea of sending fast electrical signals is fundamental to the functioning of the nervous system. In the following lessons, we’ll investigate how particular channels are able to create these action potentials in the first place.

# Channel Kinetics

The other important idea to keep in mind when thinking about how voltage-gated channels can lead to an action potential is kinetics. During an action potential, we know that the Na+ channels open first and the K+ channels open later, and this difference in timing is important because it allows positive ions to enter and leave the neuron to change the membrane potential in specific ways. In addition, the Na+ channels have two gates, the activation and inactivation gates, which also have different timings to allow these channels to be open at the correct times. ## Question 2
What would happen to the action potential if voltage-gated K+ channel activation were equally as fast as voltage-gated Na+ channel activation?
**The action potential wouldn’t initiate** is the likely intended answer. In the simplified model, if K⁺ channels open as quickly as Na⁺ channels, outward K⁺ current can oppose inward Na⁺ current from the start and prevent the positive feedback that launches the spike. Whether the spike is fully suppressed also depends on the relative sizes of the two currents.

## Question 3
The **relative refractory period** of the action potential is caused by...
**Open voltage-gated K⁺ channels**. K⁺ continues to flow out, keeping the membrane more negative. A stronger stimulus can still trigger another spike.

## Question 4
The **absolute refractory period** of the action potential is caused by...
**Voltage-gated Na⁺ channel inactivation**. Another spike cannot occur until enough Na⁺ channels recover from inactivation and become available to open again.

## Neurotoxins and Channel Function
How did neuroscientists come to understand what kinds of channels are involved in generating the action potential? Some of the mechanisms behind the action potential were discovered using ‘pharmacology’ - here, applying drugs to bind and affect ion channels. For example, it turns out nature has created a toxin that specifically blocks the voltage-gated Na+ channels: it’s called tetrodotoxin, or TTX.

TTX is found in the pufferfish, which in some parts of the world is actually a culinary delicacy since small amounts of residual toxin in prepared fish are said to produce a pleasant numbness on the lips. That numbness actually comes from the fact that it’s preventing your sensory neurons from signalling by blocking the voltage-gated Na+ channels.

TTX is an invaluable tool in neuroscience, and it’s played an important role in elucidating the inner workings of the neuron. Application of TTX to a neuron will prevent an action potential from initiating, and it’s through this observation and others that we’ve come to understand that voltage-gated Na+ channels are important for the action potential.

## Lesson 3: Conclusion

In this lesson we’ve studied the main electrical signal used by neurons to convey information: the action potential. Action potentials are all-or-nothing signals characterized by large and rapid changes in membrane potential. However, the passive properties we have discussed previously were not enough to explain how an action potential occurs. We instead investigated the active properties of neurons, specifically the existence of voltage-gated channels that open and close with particular speeds in relation to each other.

The action potential itself has many phases, and we’re able to characterize these phases by what happens during each. For example, the rising phase is defined as a time when the voltage-gated sodium channels have opened, while voltage-gated potassium channels remain closed, allowing the membrane to become depolarized (less negative) as sodium ions rush in. The falling phase is a time when the voltage-gated sodium channels have become inactivated while the voltage-gated potassium channels are opening, leading to an efflux of potassium ions and repolarization. The actions of thousands of these amazing molecular machines leads to particular ions flowing into or out of the neuron at certain times and thus gives us the characteristic action potential.

Now that we have an understanding of the action potential, a question remains: how is this electrical signal sent? This will be the next topic for discussion.

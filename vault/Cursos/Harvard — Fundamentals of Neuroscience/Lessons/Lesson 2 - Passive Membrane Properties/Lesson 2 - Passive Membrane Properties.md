# Introduction to Lesson 2: Passive Membrane Properties

So far we’ve discussed a neuron at rest, but we now want to begin considering how neurons can send electrical signals. Electrical signals can be sent through changes in a neuron’s membrane potential; the main signal we will discuss is the action potential.

However, before we begin an investigation of these electrical signals, we first need to consider the electrical properties of the neuron. How a neuron both sends and receives electrical information is governed by its physical properties, and many neurons will have vastly different electrical properties that in turn allow them to have different functions.

We will specifically examine two properties: resistance and capacitance, which you may have heard of before in the context of electronics. The resistive and capacitive properties of a neuron are governed by particular biological factors such as the number of ion channels in a neuron’s membrane, and these properties will in turn affect how the neuron responds to voltage changes.

## Question 1
When we measure the voltage across the membrane of a neuron, we are measuring the _______.
**Membrane potential**. This is the voltage difference across the neuron's membrane at any given moment. The resting potential is the membrane potential when the neuron is at rest.

## Question 2
Ions move across the membrane through channels and can thus cause changes in voltage. Each ion has its own characteristic _______ based on the inside and outside concentrations, which is an example of a(n) _______.
**Nernst potential; equilibrium**. For a single ion, the Nernst potential is the voltage at which the electrical force balances the concentration gradient. At that voltage, there is no net movement of that ion through open channels.

## Question 3
Normally, for neurons there are many ions that move across the membrane, and all of these ions’ movements balance at the ______, which is an example of a(n) ______.
**Resting potential; steady state**. At rest, individual ions can still have net movement across the membrane. The total inward and outward electrical currents balance, so the voltage stays constant while pumps maintain the concentration gradients.

# Introduction to Capacitance

Another important electrical property is that of capacitance, which can help us in thinking about the neuronal membrane. The membrane, like a capacitor, can store charges along either side and build up a voltage. Together, an understanding of both resistance and capacitance and how these concepts relate to the neuron will allow for very powerful conclusions about how neurons conduct electricity and send electrical signals. [Vídeo](videos/Introduction%20to%20Capacitance.mp4)
[Legenda do video em Português](legendas/Introduction%20to%20Capacitance.txt)

To review, we can think of the neuronal membrane as a capacitor. A capacitor builds up charge on the two sides of an insulator; in other words, there will be a build up of negative charge on one side and positive charge on the other. Capacitance refers to the ability to store charge, which for a neuron refers to the ability to store charge along the two sides of the membrane.

An increased capacitance is an increased ability to store charge. Physically, this may be due to a capacitor (i.e. the membrane) having a larger surface area. The thickness of the insulator will also affect how well positive and negative charges can attract each other and thus build up charge (or a potential) across a capacitor. In a neuron, the thickness of the insulator is the thickness of the membrane.

We also mentioned that capacitors ‘buffer’ voltage changes. In the context of the neuron, this means that if we want to apply a voltage across the membrane, this change will not happen instantly. This is because, in order to establish this voltage, charge needs to build up on either side of the membrane. In this way, capacitors ‘buffer’ and slow down voltage changes. To understand this concept further, let’s invoke the water pressure analogy from the video:

Let’s say we have two balloons, as shown in the graphic below. The left balloon is very stretchy and the right balloon is more rigid. Both balloons are being filled with water. As we are filling them up with water, we can imagine how the pressure in each balloon changes with time. Since the left balloon is stretchier and can hold more water (or charge, in the case of a neuron), we would say that it has a higher pressure capacity (or higher capacitance). So, if we fill up the balloons at the same rate, the right balloon will reach its maximum pressure more quickly than the left balloon.

[gif](images/Introduction%20to%20Capacitance.gif)

In the same way, different membrane properties can change the capacitance of the membrane. Different capacitances will affect the speed with which the membrane potential can change.

## Question 1
Which of the following would lead to a decrease in membrane capacitance of an axon? (Indicate all that apply.)
- **Decrease the diameter of the axon**. For the same axon length, a smaller diameter means less membrane surface area, which lowers total capacitance.
- **Increase the thickness of the membrane**. A thicker membrane puts more distance between the charges on its two sides, which lowers capacitance.
Think of the membrane as a capacitor: \(C = \varepsilon A/d\), where \(A\) is membrane surface area and \(d\) is membrane thickness.

## Question 1
Consider an ion for which there are only passive leakage channels. If the membrane potential is farther from the ion’s Nernst potential, the magnitude of the current associated with that ion will be...
**Greater**. The farther the membrane potential \(V_m\) is from the ion’s Nernst potential \(E_{\text{ion}}\), the larger the driving force on that ion. With constant leak conductance, a larger driving force produces a larger current magnitude:
\[
|I_{\text{ion}}| = g_{\text{ion}}\,|V_m-E_{\text{ion}}|.
\]

## Question 2
Use the circuit diagram below:
[imagem](images/Current%20and%20Ohm’s%20Law.png)

If the voltage (V) remains fixed, what will happen to the current (I) if the resistance (R) is doubled?
**The current decreases by half.** By Ohm’s law, \(I = V/R\). With voltage fixed, doubling the resistance gives \(I_{\text{new}} = V/(2R) = I/2\).

To review, the length constant represents how far a voltage change will travel until it reaches approximately 37% of its initial value. Let’s consider an example:

There’s a voltage change of 100 mV at point A. Point B is 2 mm away from point A, and point C is 6 mm away from point A. The length constant for this neuron is 2 mm.

Since B is 2 mm away from A, which is equal to one length constant, we know that the voltage change will decrease to about 37% (of the original change). So, at point B, the voltage change will be (100 mv)(0.37) = 37 mV.

C is 6 mm away from A, which is 3 length constants away. We can use the same logic and find that the voltage change at point C is (100 mv)(0.37)3 = (100 mv)(0.37)(0.37)(0.37) = ~5 mV.

So the length constant tells us information about how far voltage changes can travel in a neuron before attenuating to very small values. The 37% approximation comes from a model of exponential decay.

## Question 2
What happens to the length constant if we decrease the membrane resistance?
**Length constant decreases**. Lower membrane resistance lets more current leak across the membrane, so the voltage change fades over a shorter distance.

## Question 3
What happens to the length constant if we decrease the axial resistance?
**Length constant increases**. Lower axial resistance lets current flow more easily along the inside of the axon, so the voltage change spreads farther.
The length constant follows \(\lambda = \sqrt{r_m/r_a}\), where \(r_m\) is membrane resistance per unit length in the cable model and \(r_a\) is axial resistance per unit length.

Let's think more about what this means. When we decrease the axial resistance, it's easier for current to flow along the inside of the neuron. Ohm's Law tells us that current flows in proportion to resistance along the path of least resistance. So lowering the axial resistance means that proportionately more current will flow down the axon (the green arrows below), as opposed to flowing out through the membrane (the red arrows).

[gif](images/Resistance_in_the_Neuron_4.gif)

This means that the initial change in potential can propagate farther before returning to baseline, which, by definition, extends the length constant. So we've seen how the resistant properties of the neuron and axial plasm interact to determine how far a local change in membrane potential will propagate passively. What if we relied on this passive propagation alone to signal from one part of our body to another?

Imagine a neuron in the brain that needs to send its signal to another part of the brain just 1 centimeter away. This isn't actually very far, as axons go - some axons in the human body can extend up to a meter or more. Assuming normal, reasonable physiologic parameters for the neuron, and a length constant of 1 millimeter, how much would we need to raise the membrane potential at one end of the axon in order to get the membrane potential to rise just 15 millivolts, 1 centimeter away? That's 10 length constants away, so our signal would be attenuated by a factor of 0.37 to the 10th power. 

[imagem](images/Resistance_in_the_Neuron_5.png)

This means that we would need to raise the membrane potential 330 volts, or 330,000 millivolts, just to get a measly change of 15 millivolts on the other side.

We've already seen that generating a potential of less than 100 millivolts consumes up to a third of our daily food intake. And even if we could make the metabolic demands work out, such a large voltage would be beyond the so-called breakdown voltage for the membrane - the voltage at which the electrostatic forces are so strong that ions simply punch through the membrane. So we can't rely on passive properties alone to send our signals. There must be another way in which to get the signal from point A to point B. We'll explore how the neuron gets around this problem in the next lesson.

## Question 4
A researcher is investigating the properties of motor axons in mice, and finds that voltage changes are able to travel far down the axon without much attenuation. Which of the following explanations could lead to this result? (select all that apply)
- **The axon membrane has a small number of ion channels**. Fewer open channels mean higher membrane resistance, so less current leaks across the membrane.
- **The axon diameter is very large**. A wider axon lowers axial resistance, making it easier for current to flow along the axon. At the same channel density, axial resistance drops more sharply than membrane resistance, so the length constant increases.

## Question 5
The length of an axon is 10 mm. At one end of the axon, a voltage change of 150 mV results in a change of about 1 mV at the opposite end. Given this information, what is the approximate length constant of the neuron in mm?
**2 mm**. A passive voltage change decays exponentially with distance. After traveling 10 mm, the fraction of the original voltage change remaining is \(1/150\).
\[
\frac{1}{150}=e^{-10/\lambda}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\lambda=\frac{10}{\ln(150)}\approx 2.0\ \text{mm}.
\]
In other words, 10 mm is about five length constants in this axon.

# Lesson 2: Conclusion

In Lesson 1, we investigated the resting potential, and we’ve now begun to think about what may occur when we change the membrane potential of a neuron. A change in voltage can be used as an electrical signal. Before we delving into these electrical signals, we’ve first discussed the passive properties of a neuron. These passive properties are important to understand because they will greatly affect how a neuron is able to send and to receive electrical signals, as discussed in the next lessons.

Two main concepts that we’ve addressed in this lesson are resistance and capacitance. Resistance is opposition to the flow of current, and, for a neuron, we consider both the membrane and the axial resistance and how they can affect electrical signals. The membrane resistance describes how well current passes through the membrane while the axial resistance describes how well current passes through the cytoplasm. Capacitance is the ability to store charge, and, in the neuron, the membrane acts as a capacitor and stores charge through a buildup of ions on either side of the membrane. All of these concepts are related to each other in a famous equation: Ohm’s Law.

A consideration of both passive and, as we’ll see next, active properties, is vital for understanding how neurons are able to send electrical signals.
